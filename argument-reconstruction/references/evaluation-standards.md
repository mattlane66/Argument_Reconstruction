# Evaluation standards

Use the standard that matches each inferential step. Topic alone does not determine logical form.

## Deductive reasoning

Assess validity first: can all premises be true while the conclusion is false? If yes, provide a countermodel. If the inference is valid, assess premise truth or acceptability before classifying the argument as sound.

Use `soundness unestablished` when validity is clear but a material premise has not been verified.

## Inductive or statistical reasoning

Assess the degree of support rather than necessity. Check:

- sample size and representativeness
- selection and survivorship effects
- base rates
- measurement quality
- uncertainty and effect size
- relevant contrary cases
- whether the conclusion is calibrated to the evidence

A possible exception does not by itself defeat a probabilistic conclusion.

## Abductive or explanatory reasoning

Compare plausible rivals. Assess:

- fit with the observed evidence
- explanatory scope
- mechanism or causal intelligibility
- specificity and hard-to-vary detail
- independent or novel predictive success
- simplicity without ad hoc rescue assumptions
- consistency with established background knowledge

Hard-to-vary quality is one criterion, not a complete evaluation. An explanation can be specific yet unsupported, or broad yet well evidenced.

## Causal reasoning

Identify the causal claim and relevant counterfactual. Check:

- temporal order
- plausible mechanism
- confounding variables
- reverse causation
- selection effects
- measurement changes
- appropriate comparison or control
- intervention evidence and external validity

Do not treat sequence or correlation alone as sufficient for causation.

## Analogical reasoning

Identify the property being transferred. Compare relevant similarities, material disanalogies, and whether the proposed relation is stable across the two cases.

## Practical and policy reasoning

Make the decision rule explicit. Assess:

- goals and value priorities
- whether the proposed action advances those goals
- feasibility and implementation constraints
- alternatives, including doing nothing
- expected benefits, costs, risks, and uncertainty
- distributional effects and rights
- reversibility and opportunity cost

Empirical predictions inside a policy argument still require causal or statistical evaluation. Normative premises require separate defense.

## Moral and value-based reasoning

Identify the normative bridge. Check consistency, scope, implications, counterexamples, conflicts among values, and whether similar cases are treated similarly. Evaluate factual premises independently.

A moral argument may also be deductive, inductive, analogical, or abductive. Apply those standards to its inferential form.

## Universal checks

For every argument, ask whether the premises are acceptable, relevant, and sufficient; whether material defeaters are suppressed; and whether the conclusion changes the scope, certainty, metric, or meaning of what the premises support.

Do not let a fallacy name replace this diagnosis.
